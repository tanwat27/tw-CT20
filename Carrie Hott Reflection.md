## Class Talk:
Engaging with Carrie's talk this week really opened my eyes to all the things that are possible with Raspberry Pi computers. I actually have owned one in the past, and COMPLETELY forgot they existed. I think I was like 10 years old or so, and my dad had bought a couple of them and gave me one, basically being like, "I don't know what you're gonna do with this, but they're useful so just take it." I ended up putting retro pie on it first to play old arcade style games, eventually got bored of that and installed linux onto it to use it as a streaming device.
I didn't know until now that you could host servers and websites on them. Honestly, up until this year when I started thoughtfully engaging with technology through the CT program, I never really thought much about servers and their power usage, i.e. the whole gravity around technology and the internet in short. I've certainly never engaged with ideas about how we can minimize the power footprint when it comes to online consumption.
What I really love about this project of hers is that it sort of challenges us to think about how we don't actually need to continue on this trajectory where everything is a part of some massive project. Not only that, but it also communicates how these massive projects, like data centers, are in many ways harmful and unnecessary.
I also really loved one of the examples she shared in her talk, the low-tech magazine. She explained that it was also hosted on a solar-powered server, and that the website makes that super clear by casting a yellow film over the screen that represents how much charge the server (?) battery is at.
She explained that one of the driving goals of her solar-powered server project is to bring attention to the environmental impact of digital infrastructure. I think that the project hits that goal so well because it does it in so many ways. From the essays that came from it, the UV theming of the book, down to the tiny form factor of both the raspberry pi and its related components, as well as the solar panel itself, too. The project makes its goal visible and intelligable, which translates as a pretty powerful message. 
I'm a pretty visual-centered person, so being able to not only hear about it, but see the goal of this project in action speaks to me in a special way.
## Podcast Interview:
**Years Ago:**
I really loved the creative representation of the concept of time in this work. She talked about how she put a bunch of mugs around the table, and how they show that someone has been there. That made me think about normal everyday objects in a new light, focusing on ideas and information they can tell us subliminally. Like the mug for example. I think finding a random item in a pretty empty space is obviously going to tell you, "Hey, someone's been here," but a mug says that someone's spent time there, someone felt normal and comfortable enough in that space to enjoy a hot beverage there. I now think that mugs give meaning to the space around them, which is something I've never considered before.
